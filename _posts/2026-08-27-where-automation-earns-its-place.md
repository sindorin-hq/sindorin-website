Look for the point where useful work repeatedly waits: a planner re-entering information, a coordinator chasing approvals or a supervisor reconciling records before the next shift. Those are places to investigate an automation opportunity. Understanding why the work waits is the first task.

Repetition alone does not justify investment. The activity must be worth improving, the process must be understood and the business must be able to operate the changed workflow. Automation earns its place through the effect on the work around it.

## Choose a workflow with clear boundaries

Start with a recognisable unit of work. Define what triggers it, the information it requires and the result another person or process needs. Keep the first assessment narrow enough to see the whole sequence.

For example, processing a supplier delivery docket has a beginning and an end: the docket arrives, its details are checked against a purchase order, discrepancies are resolved and the receiving record is updated. Each step has an owner and a consequence if it goes wrong.

Walk through real examples with the people doing the work. Ask why checks exist and which variations are common. Some steps may be historical habits. Others may protect an important operational control. Understand that distinction before changing them.

Simplifying the process may remove enough effort to make automation unnecessary. It may also make the remaining automation smaller and easier to operate.

## Establish the current performance

Measure the work before selecting a solution. Useful measures include elapsed time, actual handling time, rework, backlog and the frequency of exceptions. Choose measures that describe the operational problem.

Elapsed time and handling time tell different stories. A task might require little work but wait for days between approvals. Automating data entry would address only a small part of that delay.

Find out whether the effort sits with a constrained role. Releasing a maintenance planner from repetitive administration may allow more planning to be completed. Saving scattered minutes across people who cannot use the time differently may have less commercial value.

Agree how the business would use any released capacity. That makes the benefit assessable and gives the operational owner a reason to support the change.

## Design for the exceptions

Consider a hypothetical processing business whose receiving team manually records delivery dockets. Automated capture appears promising because the documents contain familiar fields and the work repeats every day.

A closer look reveals partial deliveries, inconsistent product descriptions and dockets arriving before the purchase order has been updated. Those cases need judgement or clarification. Capturing the text will not resolve them.

A workable design might process straightforward matches and send uncertain cases to a named person with the relevant information. The assessment would then measure how many records can be completed reliably, how much review remains and whether downstream reconciliation improves.

Test representative exceptions as well as straightforward cases. Include missing information, duplicates and disagreements between records. Define what the automation should do when it cannot complete the task confidently.

An exception route must have capacity. If difficult cases accumulate in an unattended queue, the receiving team may have exchanged visible manual work for a less visible backlog.

## Include the cost of connecting and operating it

The business needs the output to arrive where it can be used. Work through integration, access, data ownership and the controls required when records are updated. Identify who maintains those connections when source systems or document formats change.

Include monitoring, support and recovery in the operating cost. Establish how staff know a task has failed, how they can correct it and what happens when the automation is unavailable.

For changes that affect equipment operation or important safety and quality controls, involve the responsible operational and engineering people from the outset. The required assurance and approvals are part of the work to be scoped.

Estimate the full effort proportionately. A narrow administrative workflow may need a modest assessment. An automation touching several operational systems may require more investigation before anyone can make a credible commitment.

## Measure the effect on the whole workflow

Check whether improvement at one step creates extra work somewhere else. Faster docket capture could increase the volume of unresolved discrepancies reaching procurement. The useful outcome is a reliable receiving record with manageable effort across the people involved.

Compare the changed process with the baseline using both normal work and exceptions. Track accuracy, completion time, manual intervention and the resulting operational benefit. Ask staff whether workarounds are developing that the measures fail to show.

Expand only when the evidence supports it and ownership is clear. A narrower scope, a different process design or a decision to defer can each be appropriate if the remaining cost outweighs the benefit.

Choose one workflow your team can describe from beginning to end. Document its delays, exceptions and current effort, then compare simplification and automation against the same outcome. That gives you a useful basis for deciding what to change and how to tell whether it worked.
